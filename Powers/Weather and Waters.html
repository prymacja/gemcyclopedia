Weather and Waters

This page groups the explicit powers assigned to gemstones in their linked articles. Each gemstone section links back to its article, where the source passages and citations are retained.

Protection from lightning, storms, and winds

Agate

Burning agate produces fumes that avert storms and halt the flow of rivers.

It also repels venomous creatures, strengthens sight, grants victory and strength, and repels tempests and lightning.

See Agate for the full entry and source citations.

Balagius

Touching the four corners of a house, garden, or vineyard with it is said to protect them from lightning, storms, and worms.

See Balagius for the full entry and source citations.

Bronia

Bronia resists lightning.

See Bronia for the full entry and source citations.

Brontea

Brontea counters the lightning-bolt.

See Brontea for the full entry and source citations.

Ceranius

Worn, Ceranius saves its bearer from drowning and protects against lightning and tornadoes.

See Ceranius for the full entry and source citations.

Chalcedony

All kinds curb lust and protect against tempests and similar adversity.

See Chalcedony for the full entry and source citations.

Coral

Coral protects the wearer at home or aboard ship. It drives away ghosts, demons, illusions, and nightmares, restrains lightning, winds, tempests, and whirlwinds, and protects against wild beasts. Coral also guards against storms and hail.

Hung among fruit trees or mixed with seed in the field, it increases fertility and keeps away hail and rain.

See Coral for the full entry and source citations.

Emerald

It halts demonic illusions and tempests, and relieves tertian fever.

See Emerald for the full entry and source citations.

Hyacinth

They guard travelers from noxious air and contagious disease, defend against lightning and enemies, bring honor and wealth, and help secure a favorable hearing from princes.

For clouded, inflamed, or suppurating eyes, hold the stone in sunlight until it warms, moisten it with saliva, then quickly lay it on the eyes.

Mermaid with a mirror and branch on sea-Hyacinth: set in a gold ring and covered with a wax seal, it makes the bearer invisible when the stone is held tightly in the palm.

See Hyacinth for the full entry and source citations.

Jasper

It is more airy than watery or fiery, and its heat varies with the clouds.

Carrying Jasper disperses phantasms and threats that appear in dreams, especially thunder and lightning.

See Jasper for the full entry and source citations.

Pearl

Thunder and lightning frighten the shells shut and can produce small, hollow, misshapen, or aborted pearls.

See Pearl for the full entry and source citations.

Thunderstone

Wear the stone in purity to be spared from lightning. During a voyage over the sea, it guards the ship from lightning and destructive whirlwinds.

See Thunderstone for the full entry and source citations.

Foretelling weather and reading the sky

Amethyst

The stone foretells southern winds, winter weather, and rainfall.

See Amethyst for the full entry and source citations.

Cimedia

Cimedia foretells calm or stormy weather in the air and at sea.

See Cimedia for the full entry and source citations.

Cinaedia

Cinaedia foretells calm or storm at sea.

See Cinaedia for the full entry and source citations.

Onyx

It forms around the third hour of the day in a thick cloud, when the sun is very hot but veiled by clouds. It is born from a ray of the sun and congealed by the clouds, and it has power against infirmities arising from the air.

See Onyx for the full entry and source citations.

Protecting fields and crops from weather

Epistrites

It drives locusts, harmful birds, clouds, hailstorms, and tornadoes away from fertile land and crops.

Thrown into boiling water, it stops the bubbling and soon cools the water.

See Epistrites for the full entry and source citations.

Hephaestite

The water cools and settles into a tranquil pool.

It shields fields from locusts, hail, whirlwinds, and rust.

See Hephaestite for the full entry and source citations.

Sea travel and navigation

Gagatromaeus

Worn by a leader going to battle, the stone grants victory by sea and land.

See Gagatromaeus for the full entry and source citations.

Magnet

The mariner’s compass is likewise turned toward the parts of the sky, helping sailors find their way when clouds or darkness hide the North Star.

See Magnet for the full entry and source citations.

Pyrophilus

Returning from India, he set the girdle aside to bathe in the Euphrates, where a serpent bit off the gem, broke it, and spat it into the river.

See Pyrophilus for the full entry and source citations.

Sagda

Sagda clings to a ship’s hull with a force compared to the Magnet’s attraction of iron.

See Sagda for the full entry and source citations.

Light, visibility, and atmospheric appearances

Haematite

Rub Haematite on darkening eyes to clear their cloud and restore sight.

See Haematite for the full entry and source citations.

Iris

It casts a brilliant, many-colored rainbow onto the opposite wall.

It is extremely transparent and dry, and some use it in place of window glass.

See Iris for the full entry and source citations.

Ophthalmius

It clouds all other eyes, so that a thief may carry away plunder openly and securely.

See Ophthalmius for the full entry and source citations.

Phengites

Cappadocian white Phengites (Fingites): Makes a windowless temple bright enough to see inside.

Clear Specularis (Mirrorstone): Quarried and split into thin sheets for windows.

One account says that a king built a windowless temple of this stone, whose transparency made the interior visible from outside.

It is quarried and split into thin sheets for windows.

It is used for windows like glass, but light pieces of fir wood serve in place of lead.

See Phengites for the full entry and source citations.

Changing water, rivers, and rain

Heliotrope

The water boils, rises as mist, clouds the air, and spills over as rain.

See Heliotrope for the full entry and source citations.

Topaz

Topaz stops boiling water from bubbling so that it soon becomes cool enough to touch.

See Topaz for the full entry and source citations.
