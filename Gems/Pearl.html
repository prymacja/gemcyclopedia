Pearl

Pearl

The image shows Pearl.JPG (source).

NamesPearl; ¹ ⁶ Pearls; Margarita; Margaritae; ⁵ Unio; ² ⁴ Union; ³ Uniones. ³
AppearanceGenerally round, smooth, and lustrous; ³ ⁴ ⁶ ⁷ white or silvery, but also alum-colored, pale, yellow, reddish, or dark with age. ¹ ² Some are pierced, flat-backed, oblong, or clustered. ¹⁴ ¹⁵
Found inIndia and the Indian Ocean, Ceylon, Stoidis, Perimula, Arabia, the Persian Gulf and Red Sea, Britain and the British Sea, Flanders, Teutonia, the Moselle, and rivers in France. ¹ ³ A separate account places pearl formation in certain salty rivers. ⁴ ⁵ ⁶ ¹⁴ ¹⁵

Pearl, also called Margarita, Unio, and Union, is a lustrous stone formed within shellfish and prized among the white gems. ¹ ² ⁴ Sea pearls are said to take their brightness and color from celestial dew, the sky, the season, and age; ² ³ ⁴ white and silvery kinds are most prized, while other pearls are yellow, reddish, or dark. ¹ ² A separate account describes pure pearls congealed from salty river waters. ⁵

Generic

Description

Pearls are formed within shellfish. The common account says the shells gape in a season and receive dew or the seed of the air; the clarity and abundance of this moisture determine the pearl’s brightness and size. Morning dew makes brighter and whiter pearls, while evening dew brings darker hues. Shellfish that receive pure dew bear brilliant pearls; cloudy or turbid conditions produce dull, pale, or reddish ones. One account says seawater hardens the pearl within the oyster’s soft flesh. ⁸ Other accounts say pearls form within the shell’s flesh or congeal from the richness and saltiness of river water. ¹ ² ³ ⁴ ⁵ ⁶ ⁷ ⁹ Spring dew yields the most worthy pearl; the whiter and brighter it is, the more effectual and virtuous it is held. ¹⁰

Properties

Pearls are soft in water and harden when brought into the air. ⁶ ⁷ ¹¹ ¹² ¹⁰ Thunder and lightning frighten the shells shut and can produce small, hollow, misshapen, or aborted pearls. ³ Their color also changes: pearls darken with age or evening air, turn yellow in age or sunlight, and may redden in the sun. ³ One account says the shell’s fill and emptying follows the waxing and waning moon.

Pearls are much prized for ornament. ⁴ The finest are clear, round, smooth, bright, and white or silver-colored; ⁴ some are alum-colored, dull gold, yellow, red, or dark. ² Some are pierced, naturally or by art; ¹ ⁴ ⁶ ⁷ ¹² others have a flat back, are oblong, or grow in clusters. The name Unio is explained by the belief that one pearl is found in a shell, though other accounts describe two, three, four, five, or even seven pearls in one shell.

Pearls are said to relieve difficulty in breathing, heart attacks, fainting, bleeding, jaundice, and diarrhea. ¹ Cooked in food, they heal quartan fevers; ⁴ ¹² macerated in milk and drunk, they heal deadly ulcers and clarify the voice. ⁴ They comfort the heart, aid fainting and epilepsy, stop the flux of the belly, and when taken with sugar help against pestilential fevers. ⁴ Wearing them is said to make a person chaste and to strengthen and harmonize body and mind.

Pearls dissolve in vinegar. ⁶ ¹² One famous account tells how Cleopatra dropped a pearl from her earring into vinegar, let it dissolve, and drank it to win a wager over the cost of her banquet.

Pearl-Bearing Shells

Pearl-bearing shells are said to swim in shoals with a leader, which divers seek because the rest of the shoal becomes motionless when it is caught. Some accounts say shellfish conceive pearls by celestial dew at night; ¹³ ⁹ ¹¹ others say they gape at certain seasons, or that their filling and emptying follows the moon.

Variants

Form Appearance and place Distinctive notes
Oriental Pearl Clear, bright, silvery white, and transparent. ⁴ The most perfect kind. ⁴
Occidental or British Pearl Found in the British seas; ⁴ dull white, tending toward gold. ⁴ Distinguished from the clearer oriental kind. ⁴
Unio A pearl said to occur singly in a shell. ² ³ The name is explained by its supposed singular occurrence, although other accounts describe several pearls in one shell. ⁴ ⁶
Naturally Perforated Pearl A pearl that remains in its shell until mature and is naturally pierced before it is cast into the sea. ⁴ Its long stay and full maturity cause it to lose its binding quality; ⁴ it is not suitable for ornament.
River-Formed Pearls Pure pearls congealed from the richness and saltiness of certain rivers. ⁵ Used to clarify water, relieve fever, and cure headache. ⁵

Subtype I: Unpierced Pearls

Those not pierced have greater binding power than naturally perforated pearls. ⁴ The account distinguishes these natural holes from pearls bored through by art.

Subtype II: Naturally Perforated Pearls

Pearls that remain long in their shells and are cast into the sea when fully mature may become naturally perforated. Their maturity causes them to lose binding quality, and they are not useful as ornaments. ⁴

Subtype III: River-Formed Pearls

These pearls form when the richness and saltiness of certain rivers settle upon sand and congeal; they are described as pure. ⁵ Place them in water, and the slime gathers around them, purifying the upper water. ⁵ A feverish person should often drink this clarified upper water. For headache, warm the pearls in the sun and tie them with a cloth around the temples.

Sources

1. St. Albertus Magnus — The Book of Minerals, Tractate II, Margarita

Open cited passage

Margarita (pearl) is a stone found in dark-coloured shells. The best come from India, but many also come from the British Sea, now called the English [Channel]; and they are also found [on the side] towards Flanders and Teutonia: so that I myself have had ten in my mouth at a single meal, which I found while eating oysters. The young shellfish have the better [pearls]. Some are pierced and some are not. Their colour is very white, but as if a little light were shining through it, and so they gleam although they are white. It is said that during a thunderstorm the oysters, miscarrying, as it were, cast them out. And so they are found in rivers, in the Moselle and some rivers in France, among the sands. Their power has been found by experience to relieve difficulty in breathing and heart attacks and fainting fits; and it is good against bleeding and jaundice and diarrhoea.

2. St. Isidore of Seville — Etymologies, Book XVI, X.1 Pearl

Open cited passage

  1. The pearl is the most excellent of the white gems. People say it is called a pearl (margarita) because this kind of stone is found in shellfish from the sea (mare), for it consists of a pebble formed in the flesh of a shellfish, like the little stone in the brain of a fish (see section 8 below). It is made from celestial dew, which shellfish absorb at a certain season of the year. A certain pearl is called a unio, an apt name because only one (unus) is found at a time, never two or more. White pearls are preferable to those that are yellow. Youth, or the reception of morning dew, makes pearls white; old age, or evening air, produces dark ones.

3. Marbodus of Rennes — De lapidibus, L. Union

Open cited stanza

The sea-born shell conceals the Union round, Called by this name as always single found. One in one shell, for ne'er a larger race. Within their pearly walls the valves embrace. Prized as an ornament its whiteness gleams, And well the robe, and well the gold beseems. At certain seasons do the oysters lie With valves wide gaping towards the teeming sky, And seize the falling dews, and pregnant breed The shining globules of th' ethereal seed. Brighter the offspring of the morning dew,. The evening yields a duskier birth to view; The younger shells produce a whiter race, We greater age in darker colours trace. The more of dew the gaping shell receives, Larger the pearl its fruitful womb conceives: However favoring airs its growth may raise. Its utmost bulk ne'er half an ounce outweighs. If thunders rattle through the vaulted sky The closing shells in sudden panic fly; Killed by the shock the embryo pearls they breed, Shapeless abortions in their place succeed.

These spoils of Neptune th' Indian ocean boasts; But equal those from ancient Albion's coasts.

4. Camillo Leonardi — Speculum Lapidum, Margarita and Unio

[[Texts/Camillo Leonardi - Speculum Lapidum#Margarita [Pearl]|Open Margarita]] · [[Texts/Camillo Leonardi - Speculum Lapidum#Margarita [Pearl]|Open Unio synonym]]

Margarita [Pearl] takes first place among the white gems and is generated by celestial dew that enters some seashells, as the authors write. This is the pearl. It is said that these shells leave the bottom of the sea in the mornings of a certain season of the year, to draw in air, from which the pearls are made. And depending on the clarity of the air element, they generate pearls that are more or less clear. The pearl’s shape is mostly round, by some called an unio, but in each shell there is no more than one. And if because of an abundance of air there is more than one in a shell, they are all globular, of which I have seen seven together, though all showed their roundness if placed against the light, and often one can see two or three of them. The most perfect are those that are silver colored with some clarity. Their size, as the learned write, does not exceed an ounce. There are two species of pearls. The first, the oriental, is of a candid color like polished silver, with a transparent surface; this is the most perfect. The second type is the occidental, which is carried by the British seas. Its color is dull with some whiteness, tending to a golden color. The oriental are the most perfect of all. And as they are big and round, they can be easily bored through by art and sometimes are naturally so, though not as regularly: these are inferior and useless as ornaments, and they are different from those that are not perforated. And this is what is said about them: the perforated pearls are more perfect and have less of a binding quality than the non-perforated. It would be ridiculous to affirm that the opinions of the most learned physicians are without meaning when they state in their recipe whether the pearl needs to be perforated or not, for when they say perforated they should clarify perforated by art: otherwise the difference is wholly to be attributed to accident and not intention. Therefore, so as not to be led into error, and to judge better than the unskilled, we should know that [naturally] perforated [pearls] are those that stayed in their shells for a long time, and when ripe are spewed out to the sea. The long stay and perfect maturity make them perforated and they thus lose their binding quality: and these are the pearls that the doctors are speaking of when they say perforated, as they are not useful for ornament and are not brought to us. But they who understand how to try them out draw comparisons between the pearls and the oak apples, because those that are not perforated have more binding power than those that are. Pearls also have physical virtues that much exceed their usefulness in ornament. These pearls still retain their virtue; if cooked in food they heal quartan fevers. If macerated in milk and drunk, they heal deadly ulcers; taken in similar fashion, they greatly clarify the voice. They comfort the heart, lend aid to those who suffer of fainting spells, and remove epilepsy. They halt the flux of the belly. If taken with sugar, they are of great aid against pestilential fevers. Whoever wears them becomes chaste.

Unio is the same as Margarita.

5. St. Hildegard of Bingen — Physica, XXI. Pearls

Open cited passage

Pearls (margaritae) are born in certain salty river-waters. The richness of these rivers falls, with saltiness, onto sand, so that the upper area of the water is purified. The richness congeals with the saltiness into pearls, and these pearls are pure.

Take these pearls and place them in water. All the slime in the water will gather around the pearls, and the top of the water will be purified and cleansed. A person who has fever should frequently drink the top of this water, and he will be better. One who ails in the head should heat pearls in the sun. He should place them around his temples, tying them with a cloth, and he will be cured.

14. Bartholomaeus Anglicanus — De proprietatibus rerum, Book XVI, Chapter 62: Margarita

Open cited source

Margarita, is chief of all white precious stones, as Isid. says, and has that name Margarita, for it is found in shells and in shell fish of the sea. It breeds in flesh of shel fish, and is sometimes found in the brain of the fish, and is gendered of the dew of heuen, the which dew shell fish receive in certain times of the year. Of the which Margarites, some be called Vniones, and have a convenable name, for only one is found, & never two or moe together. And white Margarites are better than yellow, & those that be conceived of the morrow dew, be made dim with the air of the even side. Huc vsque Isido. li. 16. And some are found kindly pierced, and those be better than other: and some be pierced by craft, as Plat. says. And they be best, that are white and cleere, bright and round. And they have virtue comfortatiue, either of all the whole kind, as some men say, or els because they are besprinkled with certain specialty, they comfort the lyins. for by constraining and coarting, they cleanse them of superfluous humours, & they help against the Cardiac passion, and against swooning or failing of heart, against feebleness that comes of the bloody flire, & against flire of the womb, as Plat. says. And in Lapidario it is said, that Margarites be gendered of dew, and the more of dew and air that is drawn in, the more and the greater they be. The Margarite is gendered of the dew, but it is supposed, that no Margarite grows passing of half a foot. Also it is said there: if that lightning or thundering fall, when the Margarite should breed of the dew that is drawn in, the shell closes by sodaine fear, and so the gendering falls and is cast out. The best Margarites come out of India, and out of the old Britain, as it is said.

15. Thomas Cantimpre — Liber de natura rerum, Book XIV: “On Precious Stones”, Margarita — Pearl

Open cited source

The pearl is found in shells. White pearls are better than pale or reddish ones. Since two are never found in one shell, they are called uniones, or “single ones.” A pearl is soft in water, but when taken out it hardens into stone. They are not found larger than half an ounce. The highest praise of their colour is that they gleam like alum. Some are oblong, while others are round. Placed in vinegar, they dissolve more quickly. This stone, ground up and prepared in the prescribed manner, cures weakness of the stomach. It also has the power to bring harmony and to produce health of mind and body—health of mind because it makes the wearer chaste.
