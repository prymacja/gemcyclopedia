Enhydros, also called Enidros or Eryndros, is a crystal-colored stone that continually exudes water without diminishing. ¹ ² ³ ⁴ It also heals burning fevers. ³

Generic

Description

Enhydros is named from water. ¹ ³ ⁴ It continually pours forth drops as if a fountain were enclosed within it, yet it does not diminish in size. One account wonders whether dew enters the stone and renews its flow, but finds the cause obscure. ² One account likewise denies that the drops come from the stone itself and explains that its great cold continually turns the surrounding air into water, especially as the weather grows warmer.

Properties

Enhydros heals burning fevers and is beneficial to those suffering from fever. ³ ⁴

Sources

1. St. Isidore of Seville — Etymologies XVI, XIII.9

Open cited source

Enhydros is named from water (cf. ὕδωρ (hydōr), “water”), for it exudes so much water that you might think there is a gushing fountain closed up in it.

2. Marbodus of Rennes — De lapidibus

Open cited source

As from full sources gush the rapid rills,
So the Enhydros ceaseless tears distils:
Obscure the cause; for if the substance flows,
How is 't the stone no diminution knows?
Nor melts away? And if external dew
Sink down within and thus the fount renew,
Would not its stream upon itself retreat
When in the pores opposing currents meet?

3. Camillo Leonardi — Speculum Lapidum

Open cited source

Enidros or Eryndros is a crystal-colored stone so called from hydros, which means water, which it perpetually exudes in small drops. Philosophers well know the reason for this: since this stone is extremely cold, it continually converts air into water with its frigidity. It heals all burning fevers.

4. St. Albertus Magnus — The Book of Minerals, Tractate II

Open cited source

Enhydros is a stone similar in colour to rock crystal. It continually distils drops, which are said to be good for those suffering from fever. But nevertheless the stone does not grow smaller or waste away. The reason for this is really that the drops do not distil from the substance of the stone at all; but because it is extremely cold, the air in contact with it continually changes into water, as often happens with hard, polished stones, when the weather gets warmer.

5. Bartholomaeus Anglicanus — De proprietatibus rerum, Book XVI, Chapter 42: Enidros

Open cited source

Enidros is a little stone, and droppeth always, and melts not, nor it is never the less in any wise, and so it is said in Lapidario: Enidros that stone, weeps always as it were by springing of a full well with dropping tears and wells always. And there it is said, that it is hard to tell the cause of these doings. For if the drops were of the substance of the stone, why is not the stone less, or melts away? And if a thing enters into the stone, why is it that that thing that enters, puts not again that thing that goes out, but as seems me, it may be, that the virtue of the stone makes the air thick that is near thereto, and turns it into water. And so it seems that it comes out of the substance of the stone. Nevertheless it comes of the substance of the air that is about the stone.

Qui velut ex pleni fontis scaturigine manat. Dissolving drops and tears full oft, that Enidros the stone does drop, Which as out of a fountaine full, does always run and never stop.

6. Bartholomaeus Anglicanus — De proprietatibus rerum, Book XVI, Chapter 101: Ydaci

Open cited source

Ydachites is a precious stone of red colour, and round in shape. And has another stone within him, and sounds by tinking thereof. Nevertheless wise men mean, that tinkling is not the inner stone, but some spirit that is within, as Dioscorides says: This stone sweats water, so that it seems that it contains a manner well spring within it self. Therefore some men tell, that this is the stone that is called Enidros. Look before in the same book.

7. Thomas Cantimpre — Liber de natura rerum, Book XIV: “On Precious Stones”, Clidros (Enhygros)

Open cited source

Clidros is a stone resembling crystal, which in a wonderful manner continually drips tears. More wonderful still is that the substance of the stone does not dissolve, nor does the stone diminish; rather, it remains hard and entirely intact in every respect.
