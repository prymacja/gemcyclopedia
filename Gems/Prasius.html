Prasius, also called Prasine or Prase, is a green stone named for the leek-like hue of its color. ¹ Some kinds bear blood-red spots or three white lines. ¹ ³ It treats burning fever and bruises when prepared and applied as prescribed, though its virtue is also denied. ²

Generic

Description

Prasius is a green stone whose hue is likened to the leek and the Emerald. ¹ One kind is plain green, another is marked with blood-red spots, and a third bears three white lines. ¹ ³

Prasius forms as evening approaches, when the sun withdraws and dew nears. ² The setting sun heats the mountain stone; ² from the sun’s warmth, the humidity of air and water, and the force of dew, the stone is born. ²

Properties

For a burning fever, roll Prasius in a little rye-bread dough, tie it in cloth, and keep it over the navel for three days and nights; ² the fever departs.

For a bruise from a blow or fall, mix old fat with equal amounts of sage and tansy, press Prasius into the mixture, and heat it in the sun or near a fire. ² Apply the warmed mixture together with the stone over the injured place, and it will improve.

One account calls Prasius a base stone; ¹ another says it has no virtue beyond its beauty. ³

Subtype I: Green Prasius

Prasius is named for its leek-green color. ¹ ² Another account describes it as emerald-green and values it for its beauty. ³ The remedies for Prasius are given without a color form, so they are not limited to this green kind. ²

This kind is green with blood-red spots. ¹ ³

Sources

1. St. Isidore of Seville — Etymologies, Book XVI, VII.4

Open the cited text

Prasius is named for its green color (cf. πρασώδης (prasōdēs), “leek-green”), but it is a base stone. Another variety of it is marred by blood-like spots. A third variety is distinguished by its three white lines.

2. St. Hildegard of Bingen — Physica, XI. Prasine

Open the cited text

Prasine (prasius) develops around eventide, when the sun withdraws its rays from the upper parts of the earth, and when the dew is approaching. The sun gradually falls over the stone of the mountain and heats it up greatly. From the heat of the sun, the humidity of the air and water, and the vigor of the dew, the prasine is born.

One who has a burning fever should roll a prasine in a bit of rye bread dough, and tie it with a cloth. For three days and nights he should keep it tied over his umbilicus, and the fever will go from him.

But, if someone is bruised on any part of his body from a blow or a fall, he should take old fat, and mix with it equal amounts of sage and tansy. He should press prasine into it, then heat it in the sun or near the fire. Then he should place all this, with the stone, so heated, over the place where it hurts, and it will be better.

3. Marbodus of Rennes — De lapidibus, XL. Prase

Open the cited text

Midst precious stones a place the Prase may claim, Of value small, content with beauty's fame. No virtue has it; but it brightly gleams With emerald green, and well the gold beseems; Or blood-red spots diversify its green, Or crossed with three white lines its face is seen.

4. Thomas Cantimpre — Liber de natura rerum, Book XIV: “On Precious Stones”, Parasius, or Prasius

Open cited source

Prasius is a beautiful green stone, but its colour is denser than that of emerald. It is of no use except that it looks attractive in gold. Emerald is cut from this stone.
