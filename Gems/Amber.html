Amber, also called Succinus, Succinum, Electrum, Sucinus, Lambra, Harpaga, and Karabe, is a yellow tree gum that hardens into a gem. ¹ ² ³ It may be clear as glass or tawny like wax, sometimes with blue and saffron hues. It attracts light objects when warmed by rubbing, and its smoke drives away serpents and other poisonous creatures. ³

Generic

Description

Amber is the hardened gum of a tree like the pine. ² ³ It is yellow and may be transparent as glass; ¹ another kind is tawny like wax, sometimes with a blue tint mixed with saffron color. ² The gum hardens in the sun, in cold, and with time, trapping whatever it meets as it runs; small animals and straw may thus be enclosed within it. ³ The best kind flows in hot summer, while darker amber comes from the juice of other seasons. Amber hardened on trees near the shore may be shaken into the sea, where it hardens further and becomes polished before storms bring it back to the coast, where it can be caught in nets.

Properties

  • Warmed by rubbing on cloth, amber attracts leaves, straws, threads, chaff, and cloth fringes, as the Magnet attracts iron. ¹ ² ³
  • Worn, it makes its bearer chaste. When burnt, it drives away serpents; its smoke also drives away poisonous creatures and its vapors repel epilepsy. ¹ ³
  • Amber halts flux of the belly, remedies infirmities of the throat, repels poison, and holds loose teeth fast. Women and children wear amber necklaces to ward off throat ailments. ³
  • It helps pregnant women to an easy birth. Taken inwardly, it increases the flow of urine and quickens menstruation as well as easing childbirth. ¹ ³
  • Placed upon a wife's left breast while she sleeps, amber is said to make her confess her misdeeds. ³ To test whether a woman has been sexually unfaithful, the stone is left in water for three days and she is made to drink it; ³ the text says that if she has been unfaithful, it will force her to urinate.

Varieties

Subtype Appearance or origin Properties
Subtype I: Summer Amber The better kind, formed from tree juice flowing in the heat of summer. ¹ No distinct power is assigned.
Subtype II: Dark Amber Darker amber formed from juice flowing in other seasons. ¹ No distinct power is assigned.

A further account distinguishes three kinds of electrum: a tree gum that hardens into a clear stone, native electrum metal, and a gold–silver alloy. The warmed tree-gum kind attracts leaves, straw, and garment fringes; the warning against poison belongs to electrum as the account describes it. ⁴

Sources

1. St. Albertus Magnus — The Book of Minerals, Tractate II

Open cited source

[Succinus] (amber) is a stone of a yellow colour, which the Greeks call [electrum]. Sometimes it is found as transparent as glass. The name comes from the material, for it is made of the juice (succus) or gum of a tree called pine. A popular name for it is [lambra]. If rubbed it attracts leaves, straws, and threads, as the Magnet [attracts] iron. They say that it makes those that wear it chaste. Experience shows that if burnt it drives away serpents; and it helps pregnant women to an easy birth. The better kind is formed from the juice that runs out in the hot summer; the darker kind, from the juice of the other [seasons].

2. St. Isidore of Seville — Etymologies, Book XVI

Open cited source

Amber (sucinus), which the Greeks call ἤλεκτρον (ēlektron), has the color of tawny wax, and is said to be the sap (sucus) of trees, and for the reason is called ‘amber.’ An explanation taken from myth has led it to being called electrum, for the story goes that when Phaethon was killed by a bolt of lightning, his sisters, in their grief, were turned into poplar trees, and they exude amber (electrum) as tears, year in and out, by the river Eridanus (i.e. the Po); and it is called electrum because many poets said the sun used to be called Elector (the ‘Shining One’). But in fact amber is not the sap of the poplar tree but of the pine tree, for when it is burned it gives off the fragrance of pine pitch.

3. Camillo Leonardi — Speculum Lapidum

Open cited source

Succinum [Amber], which belongs to the gagates species, as said before, even though it is a gum, nonetheless it is listed among the gems because of its beauty and the use ancient people made of it. It is of a transparent yellow with a sort of blue tint within mixed with saffron color. Pliny reports how much the ancients esteemed it. It is said to be the gum from a tree by the same name that is similar to a pine tree. However, it clear that it is not the gummi populi as the poets suggest based on Phaeton’s fable. It is found in many locations, such as Dacia, England, and Brittany. But it is most abundant along the Northern Ocean, opposite Gessaria Island, which the Germans also call Amber Island. This gum solidifies under the sun, with the cold and with the passing of time. Since it oozes out of the trees at various times, it traps inside it anything it encounters in its path. Thus at times we can find enclosed within it small animals and straw; in fact, sometimes counterfeiters will soften succinum and place something within. When this gum is hardened on trees, and if these are close to the shore, the wind shaking the branches makes the amber fall into the sea. Here it hardens further and becomes more polished; finally, it is brought to the shores by sea tempests and can be caught with nets. Just as the Magnet attracts iron, so does heated amber attract straw, if rubbed on a cloth. Its virtues are the same as those of the gagates, although more numerous and powerful. Its nature halts the flux of the belly. Similarly, it is an efficacious remedy for all the infirmities of the throat. Thus the ancients, to avoid these illnesses, prescribed women and children to wear [amber] necklaces. It repels poison. And if placed on a wife’s left breast, while she sleeps, it makes her confess all the ill deeds she committed. If ingested, it increases the urine flow, quickens the menstrual flow, and eases birth. It holds loose teeth fast. Its smoke drives away poisonous [animals]. If one wishes to discover if a woman has been corrupted, he must leave the amber in water for three days and have her drink it. If she has been corrupted, she will immediately be forced to urinate.

Karabe is the same as Succinum, of which more will be said later; some [learned men] think that they are different, though they are not different in color or in virtue. But its vapors chase away epilepsy.

4. Bartholomaeus Anglicanus — De proprietatibus rerum, Book XVI, Chapter 38: Electro

Open cited source

Electrum is a metal, and has that name, for in the Sun beam it shines more cleere then gold or silver. For in the Sun beam Electrum is effectual. And this metal is more noble then other metals. And heer of be three manner of kinds: One is such, that when it runs first out of the tree, it is fleeting and thin Gumm, but afterward with heat or with cold it is made hard as a cleere stone, as it were christal. That other manner kind is called metal, & is found in the earth, and in and in price, The third manner is made of the three parts of gold, & of the fourth of silver: and kind Electrum is of that kind, for in twinckling & in light it shines more cleere then all other metal: & warns of venom: for if one deep it therein, it makes a great chirping noise, & changes oft into diverse colours, as the rainbow, & that suddenly. Huc vsque Isi. lib. 15. de Metallis vlti Et super Eze. is mention shade of Electrum artificiall. Greg. says, that if hard gold be mingled with silver, then the filtier wiss more cleere, & the gold tempered with cloerenesse of silver, looses not his virtue, but only it wars somewhat pale, & less fair by mingling of silver. And some men cal very Glectrum Arpago. For if it be heted with fingers, it draws to it self all leaves & straws, & hems of clothes, as Magnis draws iron, as Isid says; li. 16. ca. de rubiis Gemis. And receives soon colour & hue, in what manner a man will. And so it is soon died with the rote of Auense, with Couchinello, as he eth there.

5. Thomas Cantimpre — Liber de natura rerum, Book XIV: “On Precious Stones”, Succinus (Ambra) — Amber

Open cited source

Amber, which the Greeks call electron, is saffron-coloured and sometimes shines or is translucent like glass. According to Isidore, it is the sap of a pine tree, and for this reason it is called succinus; in common speech it is also called ambra. When rubbed or warmed between the fingers, it attracts leaves, straws, and the fringes of garments, just as the magnet attracts iron. It may be handled in whatever way one wishes, for it is dyed with the root of a certain herb called attusa. It is said to confer chastity on those who wear it. Its smoke is said to help women in labour and to drive away serpents.
