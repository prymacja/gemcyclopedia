Emerald

Emerald

Green emerald specimen (source).

NamesSmaragdus, Emerald, Green Stone, Galacidem. ¹ ² ³ ⁴ ⁵ ⁶; Smaragdo ⁷
AppearanceIntensely green and translucent, sometimes reflective, yellowish, spotted, marked with black rods, or mixed with Chalcedony. ¹ ² ³ ⁵ ⁷ ⁸
Found inScythia, Britain, the Nile, India, Ethiopia, Phison, the Red Sea, Emerald Island, Beronike, Pelmi, Bactria, Egypt, Carchedonia, Persia, and Babylon. ¹ ² ³ ⁵ ⁶ ⁷ ⁸

The emerald is usually intensely green and translucent. ¹ ³ ⁵ ⁶ ⁷ ⁸ Some kinds are yellowish. ² The finest specimens keep their brightness in sunlight and shade; ¹ ⁵ ⁶ ⁷ ⁸ a smooth, polished surface can reflect images like a mirror. ³ ⁵ ⁶ ⁷ The emerald strengthens sight and memory, encourages chastity and eloquence, increases wealth, and protects against illness, poison, demons, and tempests. ¹ ⁵ ⁶ ⁸

Generic

Description

The emerald’s green is so vivid that it seems to tint the surrounding air. ² ³ ⁵ ⁶ ⁷ ⁸ Some kinds are yellowish; others have black rods or spots, contain chalcedony, or occur in copper veins. ¹ ² The traditions count either twelve or eight kinds; their named and regional forms are listed below. ¹ ² ⁵

Properties

Sight, reflected images, and foresight

The emerald reflects the viewer’s face like a mirror; a broad, polished surface may show images. ² ³ ⁵ Its green comforts gem-cutters’ eyes, ¹ ³ ⁶ restores fatigued sight, and sharpens vision. ¹ ⁶ It gives foresight, aids divination, and reveals the outcome of cases to be heard. ¹

Chastity, memory, speech, and fortune

The emerald inclines its wearer to chastity and checks lustful desire. ¹ ⁶ It breaks if its wearer has sexual intercourse; it also breaks if a virgin wearing it loses her virginity. ¹ It tempers anger and lust and makes its wearer gracious to God and men. ¹ It increases wealth, ¹ ⁵ ⁶ ⁸ sharpens memory, ¹ ² and strengthens argument and persuasive speech. ¹ ⁶

Healing and protection

Worn about the neck, it cures hemitertian fever and epilepsy, strengthens weak sight, preserves the eyes, and improves memory. ¹ Carried on the person, it relieves weakness and illness of the heart, stomach, and side. ⁴ During a sudden pestilential attack, moisten it in the mouth and touch it repeatedly to the body. ⁴ During an epileptic attack, hold it to the sufferer; to cure epilepsy, look upon it on nine successive mornings and say the prescribed prayer. ⁴

For severe headache, warm the stone with the breath, rub it over the temples and forehead, and hold it in the mouth. ⁴ To purge the head and lessen phlegm and saliva, pour warm wine over it repeatedly and drink the wine with broad beans. ⁴ Bound over a wound for three days, it kills worms. ⁴

The emerald counters poisonous bites, especially those of a rabid dog. ⁶ In powder, it serves against leprosy, kidney stones, and poison. ⁵ ⁶ It halts demonic illusions and tempests ⁵ ⁶ and relieves tertian fever. ⁵ ⁶

Engravings

An image of a merchant carrying goods, or a man seated beneath a centurion, brings wealth, victory, and freedom from distress and evil. ⁶ An armed horseman with a lance gives victory on the battlefield. ⁶

Sources

1. St. Albertus Magnus — The Book of Minerals, Tractate II, Chapter 17: “Smaragdus”

Open the cited text ↗

Smaragdus is a stone more precious than many others, and it is not rare. Its colour is very green and translucent, so that it seems to tinge the air around it with its own greenness. The best shape for it is smooth on the surface, for then one part does not cast a shadow on another part. And the best do not change [colour] in light and shade. They say that there are twelve varieties, depending on differences in their smoothness and colour; for sometimes it has a kind of black gall, like little rods in it. Some varieties are named from their places of origin—those of Scythia, or of Britain, and of the Nile; and [then there are] those that occur in veins of copper; and some that are spotted; and some [called] 'Chalcedonian', containing a mixture of that stone chalcedony. The best of all are those of Scythia. It is reported that they are taken from the nests of griffins, which guard this stone with great ferocity. And a traveller from Greece, a truthful man and a careful observer, has said that this stone occurs in submarine ledges of rock, and that it is frequently found there. A reasonable explanation is that it occurs in veins of copper, and is transparent because it has not yet actually become copper; for the 'rust' of copper [i.e. verdigris] is green. It has been found by experience in our own time that this stone, if it is good and genuine, will not endure sexual intercourse: because the present King of Hungary wore this stone on his finger when he had intercourse with his wife, and as a result it was broken into three pieces. And therefore what they say [of this stone] is probable—that it inclines the wearer towards chastity. They say, too, that it increases wealth, and confers persuasive speech in [pleading] causes; and suspended from the neck, cures hemitertian fever and epilepsy. And it has been found by experience to strengthen weak sight and to preserve the eyes. They say also that it improves the memory, and averts tempests, and is good for divination; and therefore it is sought after by magicians.

2. St. Epiphanius — On the Twelve Gemstones, third stone (Levi’s tribe)

Emerald, which is also the Green one, is yellowish in appearance. Between its types there are differences, for one of them is called Neronianos, the other Dometianos. It is bitter, very yellowish in appearance, and brightly transparent. But it is called Neronianos, as one says, for this reason: They say the mountain was soaked with the oil of King Nero, which is Spanish yellow or libertine, although others say: almond-colored. But others say susinian oil soaked the mountain. But others say: No.

↗ Open the matching source passage

Rather, they say the oil was filled in metian copper vessels and given to the Nicaeans. And from the copper rust the oil became yellowish over time and thus nourished the mountain. And as the oil was absorbed by the rock, it bloomed ever more in yellowishness and transparency. And others say: No. Rather, Neron was the name of a certain pinaropic artist, or a jeweler, who knew which of the emeralds is the noblest. And accordingly one calls it "Neronianos." That means: "new in splendor."

↗ Open the matching source passage

For Dometianus ruled as king; he was the son of Vespasianus, who conquered Jerusalem; who in turn was the brother of Titus. And Nero and Dometianus were selfish-proud kings.

Therefore Holy Scripture sings, saying that it is found near the river Phison: "Phison is the one that covers the whole land of Evilat." For there lies the Green Stone. It is also said of the Ruby that it is from there.

↗ Open the matching source passage

And this river Phison is called 'Indian river' according to the language of the Greeks; but according to the barbarian peoples it is called 'Ganges' among the Evilaeans and Brachmans, among the Geziners and Bugaeans. For many of the Greek historians also know the river Ganges, which flows through the whole land of Evilat, by that name. But 'Evilaeans' the natives call the Indians. And flowing through it reaches toward evening the land called Ethiopia and empties into the ocean. Therefore they also say that the ruby stone was found at the borders of this river, which flows into the lands of the Libyans, where in earlier times those lived who were called Ethiopians.

And finding somewhere the Hebrew designation of this stone, he translated it as Bodali, instead of the corresponding Jewish Green Stone. Just as one also says, "there was the Green Stone." Aquila says, "there was the Bdelion." But there is Bdelion, either the anointing oil or the incense that is brought from India.

But the varieties of emeralds are eight that are found of these. And the nature of these is beautifully colored, intense and soft colored. They are from the lands of the Indians and Ethiopians.

For once nine kings ruled according to the historical account over the Alabastrians, Homeritans, Axometans together with Adulitans, Tanisnaziians, Asabenoneons, Dirnonians and Helenkibenonians. But now there are also many more divisions: For earlier the Dibeners were one with the Fish-eaters, and the Sirindibeners with the Zakamtazins, and the Elendibeners with the Evilaeans. 3. St. Isidore of Seville — Etymologies, Book XVI, VII.1–4

Open the cited text ↗

Of all green gems, the smaragdus (i.e. emeralds, etc.) holds preeminence. The ancients assigned it third place in value after the pearl and the unio (i.e. a type of pearl, see X.1 below). The smaragdus is named from its extreme greenness, for everything that is very green is called ‘bitter’ (amarus). No gem or plant possesses greater intensity than the smaragdus; it exceeds green plants and leaves and imbues the reflected air around it with greenness. Nothing is more soothing to the eyes of gem cutters than this refreshing green. If its surface is broad, it reflects images like a mirror. Indeed the Emperor Nero used to watch gladiator matches using a smaragdus.

4. St. Hildegard of Bingen — Physica, Stones, I: “Emerald”

Open the cited text ↗

The emerald (smaragdus) grows in the wee hours of morning at sunrise, when the sun is powerfully placed in its orbit, traversing its route. Then the natural vigor of the earth and grasses is especially lively, the air still cold, the sun hot. The herbs vigorously imbibe vitality, as a lamb sucking milk. The heat of the day is scarcely sufficient to dry that day’s vital energy, and it gives nourishment so the plants become fertile and produce fruit. Therefore, the emerald is powerful against all human weakness and sickness, since the sun readies it, and since all its matter is of the vitality of the air.

↗ Open the matching source passage

Whence, one who ails in his heart, stomach, or side should have an emerald with him. It will heat up his flesh, making him better. If this person is so beset by pestilences which cannot restrain their commotions, he should place an emerald in his mouth, so it becomes wet from his saliva and the saliva heats up from the stone. He should repeatedly place it on, and take it from, his body. The sudden pestilential attacks will cease, without a doubt.

↗ Open the matching source passage

When someone tormented by epilepsy falls, put an emerald in his mouth while he is lying prostrate. It will revive his spirit. After he gets up and takes the stone from his mouth, he should look at it intently, and say, “Just as the breath of the Lord filled the whole earth, so may his grace fill the house of my body, so that it can never be moved.” He should do this in the morning for the next nine days, and he will be cured. He should always have that stone with him and look at it every morning while saying those words, and he will be healed.

↗ Open the matching source passage

One who has great pain in his head should hold it near his mouth and warm it by his breath, so that it becomes damp from his breath. He should rub it, so dampened, on his temples and forehead. Then he should put it in his mouth and, holding it in his mouth for a little while, he will be better.

↗ Open the matching source passage

Someone who has much phlegm and saliva in him should heat good wine and then put a linen cloth over a metallic vessel. He should rest an emerald on that cloth and pour the warm wine over the stone, so the wine passes through the cloth. He should do this again and again, as if preparing lye. Then he should frequently eat this wine, mixed with ground broad beans, and drink the wine itself. It will purge his head and diminish the phlegm and saliva.

↗ Open the matching source passage

If someone is being eaten by worms, he should place a linen cloth, with an emerald on top, over the wound. He should tie over it other small pieces of cloth, so that the stone grows warm. If this is done for three days, the worms will die.

5. Marbodus of Rennes — De lapidibus, section VII

Open the cited text ↗

Of all green things which bounteous earth supplies
Nothingin greenness with the Emerald vies;
Twelve kinds it gives, sent from the Scythian clime,
The Bactrian mountain, and old Nilus' slime;
And some from copper mines of viler race
Marked by the dross drawn from their matrix base:
The Carchedonian from the Punic vale —
To name the othere were a tedious tale.
From all the rest the Scythian bear the palm
Of higher value and of brighter charm,
From watchful gryphons in the desert isle
Stol'n by the vent'rous Arimaspian's guile.
Higher their value which admit the sight.
And tinge with green the circumambient light:
Unchanged by sun or shade their lustre glows,
The blazing lamp no dimness on it throws.
Such as a smooth or hollow surface spread
Like slumbering ocean in its tranquil bed.
These like a mirror the beholder's face
Exactly image with reflected rays:
And thus did Nero, if report say true,
The mimic warfare of the arena view.
But best the gem that shews an even sheen,
Lustrous with equal never-varying green.
Of mighty use to seers who seek to pry
Into the future hid from mortal eye.
Wear it with reverence due, 'twill wealth bestow
And words persuasive from thy lips shall flow,
As though the gift of eloquence inspired
The stone itself or living spirit fired.
Hung round the neck it cures the ague's chill.
Or falling sickness, dire mysterious ill;
Its hues so soft refresh the wearied eye,
And furious tempests banish from the sky:
So with chaste power it tames the furious mood
And cools the wanton thoughts that fire the blood.
If steeped in verdant oil or bathed in wine
Its deepened hues with perfect lustre sliine.

6. Camillo Leonardi — Speculum Lapidum, Book II, Chapter 7 (“Smaragdus”), with related passages in Book III

Open the cited text ↗

Smaragdus [Emerald]. There are many types, but the Scythians are superior to all. Their green is so intense that placed under a light or the rays of the sun, not only does their color not dim but rather its green, intensified in strength, coloring the surrounding air with its hue. From this it takes its name, because everything that is so deeply green may be called an emerald. I find that lapidaries describe twelve species, though, as we mentioned, the Scythian kind is more precious and noble then the others. After this, it follows the British, the Egyptian, the Hermitian, the Persian, and those that are found in the copper mines. Even though they are all transparent, they differ in the intensity of green. Its green is so delightful that certainly no other gem delights the eyes as much. When its surface is evened out [polished], then they reflect images as mirrors do. It is said that the Emperor Nero had an emerald of marvelous size through which he saw gladiators’ battles. There are other species that vary in color, with varied small spots, which are called fake emeralds; these, along with the aforementioned, add up to twelve. The six species mentioned above, though, are the only ones of great value. There are some big ones, but not as big as fake emeralds; however, Pliny writes of an obelisk that was fifty cubits high, of another that it was four cubits high, and of another two in the Temple of Jupiter in the Babylonian kingdom that was made of four emeralds. Theophrastus, too, reports having seen an emerald that was four cubits in size. It is also said that in Rome there used to be a large [emerald] sphere in the Temple of Hercules. But, as we said before, there are not many perfect specimens. The emerald surface shape is flat and thus hides its faults because its color is equally fulgent and images may be impressed on it. This stone is useful to those who love chastity, thus it does not tolerate that a virgin be defloured, for then it breaks, as Albertus reports. It stops lascivious inclinations. It increases wealth. It halts demons’ illusions and tempests. It heals tertian fevers and propitiates the wearer to god and men. It opposes epilepsy, sharpens memory, and lends strength to the arguments of rhetoricians, and it is useful for them, as they worry about foreknowledge of future cases. It restores fatigued sight and renders it sharper. It is useful against poisonous bites, especially those of a rabid dog. It is the best remedy for leprosy, kidney stones, and poisons, if taken pulverized.

Galacidem is the same as Smaragdus.

7. Bartholomaeus Anglicanus — De proprietatibus rerum, Book XVI, Chapter 88: Smaragdo

Open cited source

Smaragdus of al green precious stones is the chief, as Isidore says. Men in old time gave thereto the third dignity after Margarites and unions. Smaragdus has that name of green colour, as he said there. For it is said that all green things is bitter. In no herbs nor in precious stone is more greenness then in the stone Smaragdus. It passes herbs & grass, twigs & braunches: And infects the air about it with passing green colour: And his green colour abates not in the Sun in any manner wise. Nothing comforts more their eyes that be engravers, then this stane: If the body thereof be straight and cleansed or polished, then Images be seen therein as it were in a mirror. Cesar Nero used to see fighting of sword players in this stone, as Isidore says. Thereof be 12. manner of kinds, but the most noble are found in Scythia, and in Bactria holds the second place: and Smaragdis be found among & under stones, and in chinnes thereof, when the Northern wind blows, for then the earth is vncouered, and Smaragdis shines among the stones, for in such wind gravel and sand is most moved. The Egyptians have the third. Other be found in metall or ore of brass, but they be gleyming, for they have speckles like to brass, or to lead, or to salt. Though the Smaragdus be green by kind, yet if it be mingled with wine or with oil, his green colour increases. There is a manner Smaragdus that goes out of kind though it be green, for it is somewhat unseemly by beines of brass, and is called Calcesmaragdus. Hue vsque Isid. li. ca. de viridibus gemmis. This stone is taken of and from Griphons, and plenty of Smaragdus may not be found: for great Griphons let the coming of men by the way that goes thereto, as Isid. says li. 13. cap. 3. And this stone multiplies his green colour, of him comes a beam that dies the air about him, and makes it green. The body thereof is cleere and of glassy kind, & shows figures, images and shapes of things that be near thereto, and has of gift of kind & goodness of virtue to heal diverse sicknesses and euills. Dioscorides says; it increases riches, & makes men have good words and fair evidence, in caose and in plea. If this stone be hanged about the neck, it helps the falling evil, and saves and comforts feeble sight, and represseth wanton motions of lechery, and makes good mind, and helps also against al fantasies & japes of fiends, and ceases tempest, and staunches blood: and it is said, that it helps them, that use to divine and gess what shall befall, as it is said in Lapid.

8. Thomas Cantimpre — Liber de natura rerum, Book XIV: “On Precious Stones”, Smaragdus — Emerald

Open cited source

Emerald is a precious stone, one of the twelve. It is greener than anything else that is green. The best is found in Scythia, where the Arimaspi take it from griffins. The finest stones are those through which the eye can see, whose radiance makes the neighbouring air appear green, and whose greenness is not dimmed by sun, lamplight, or shade. The best shape is flat.

If worn chastely, it drives away the falling sickness, which is called li entericus. It refreshes the sight and cleanses the eyes. If it is washed with wine or anointed with oil, it makes a person pleasing in speech. It increases riches, helps those who investigate hidden matters, averts storms, and restrains lustful impulses.
