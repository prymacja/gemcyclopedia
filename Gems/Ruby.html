Ruby

AppearanceHard, clear, and red; ¹ carbuncle shines with violet or coal-like fire. ³ ⁵ ⁶ Some forms glow by night, and some spark intermittently. ² ⁷ ⁸
Found inLibya, among the Troglodytes; ¹ ³ ⁶ Karchedon in Africa; ² India; and the river Phison. ² ⁷ ⁸

Ruby, also called Carbuncle, Anthrax, Rubinus, and Sparks, is a hard, clear red stone whose fiery rays shine like a coal in the darkness. ¹ ³ ⁵ ⁶ A noble ruby glows by night; ² ³ ⁴ its radiance can reveal it even through clothing. Libya and the land of the Troglodytes yield carbuncles, while other accounts place ruby in Karchedon and the river Phison. ²

Generic

Description

The carbuncle is ranked foremost among fiery gems. ⁵ ⁶ It is born during a lunar eclipse, when the sun’s heat revives the moon. It is hard, clear, and red, shining with rays like a burning coal; ¹ ⁵ ⁶ at night it can cast its flames toward the eye. ³ The brilliant kinds are most noble. One truly fine ruby shines by itself in the dark; a lesser but genuine stone glows when clear water is poured over it in a clean, polished black vessel. ¹ A stone that does not shine in darkness is not of perfect quality. ¹

There are said to be twelve kinds, though one account names eleven, and another lists only three kinds: Balas Ruby, Garnet, and Rubinus. ¹ ⁶ A further classification names five fiery gems: Carbuncle, Ruby, Balas, Spinel, and Granate. ⁶ The Rubinus is a species of carbuncle, distinguished chiefly by its smaller size; a darker kind is of little value. ⁶ These classifications overlap but do not describe every kind in the same way.

The carbuncle does not suffer from fire and does not absorb the color of nearby gems, though other stones absorb its light. Its female kind gives forth more light. ⁶

Properties

The carbuncle’s chief virtue is to disperse poison in the air or vapor. ¹ It also drives away pestilential and poisonous air, quiets lust, preserves bodily health, banishes evil thoughts, reconciles friends who quarrel, and increases prosperity. ⁶

For any illness that has changed a person’s humors, place the carbuncle on the navel at midnight. ⁴ Remove it as soon as the person feels warmth from the stone or the slightest motion in the body. ⁴ If left longer, its power penetrates the whole body and dries it, including the internal organs.

For a head ailment, place the stone briefly on the crown. Remove it as soon as the flesh warms; ⁴ its strength then penetrates the head and brings relief. ⁴

Placed in clothing or another object, the carbuncle makes it last longer and decay with difficulty. ⁴ Airy spirits cannot complete their phantasms where the stone is present; they flee from it. ⁴

Ruby’s light shines intermittently at night, and sparks lead the finder to the stone. It bears slight resemblance to Keraunios, also called the wine-like Keronion or Karchedonios. The two stones are found in the same place; Keraunios is called “not delightful.” ² Its radiance remains visible through many layers of clothing, so it cannot be hidden.

Sources

1. St. Albertus Magnus — The Book of Minerals, Tractate II

Open the cited text

Carbunculus (carbuncle), which is anthrax in Greek, and is called rubinus (ruby) by some, is a stone that is extremely clear, red, and hard. It is to other stones as gold is to the other metals. It is said to have more powers than all other stones, as we have already said. But its special effect is to disperse poison in air or vapour. When it is really good it shines in the dark like a live coal, and I myself have seen such a one. But when it is less good, though genuine, it shines in the dark if clear limpid water is poured over it in a clean, polished black vessel. One that does not shine in the dark is not of perfect, noble quality. It is mostly found in Libya; and although there are several varieties, so that Evax says that there are eleven kinds, nevertheless Aristotle, according to Constantine, says that there are only three kinds which we have enumerated above—namely, balagius, granatus, and rubinus. And—what surprises many people—he says that granatus is the most excellent of these; but jewellers consider it less valuable.

2. St. Epiphanius — On the Twelve Gemstones, The Fourth Stone: Ruby

Open the cited text

The Ruby, whose appearance is strongly red. It is found in Karchedon of the Libyans, whose name is Africa. But some say that this stone was found as follows: During the day one did not see it, but at night it was perceived from afar, similar to a lamp. Or the Ruby was perceived because of its sparkling nature. And there is an hour when this Ruby shines, and an hour when its rays cease to sparkle. Whereby this appears puzzling to those who observe it from afar; for it is sometimes seen, and sometimes it ceases: But through this they recognize that this is this stone. Following the shining sparks of this stone, one finds it. But whoever possesses it has no possibility to hide it. Even if he tries to hide it through many clothes, it will be seen through the clothing because of its shining sparkle. Consequently it was called "Sparks" for these reasons.

And this one has a slight resemblance to the stone Keraunios. Some call it 'wine-like Keronion.' Or it is also called Karchedonios because in its appearance it is similar to this stone. For it was found like this one in the same place. But it is not delightful.

And Holy Scripture says that this comes from the river Phison, although it is also to be found in Africa. For the river Phison flows into the sea, from the southern side toward the western ocean through all places. And as the stone falls from the western side into the river Phison and foams up here, one sees it from the side of the Libyans.

But Holy Scripture says: "A river flows from Eden, and from there it divides into four parts. One is called Phison. This is the one that surrounds the whole land of Evilat, where gold is. The gold of this land is good. There is the stone Ruby and the Green one."

3. St. Isidore of Seville — Etymologies, Book XVI, XIV.1

Open the cited text

Of all the fiery gems, the carbuncle (carbunculus) holds the principal rank. It is called ‘carbuncle’ because it is fiery, like a coal (carbo), and its gleam is not overcome by the night, for it gives so much light in the darkness that it casts its flames up to the eye. There are twelve kinds, but the most outstanding are those that seem to glow, as if giving off fire. The carbuncle is called ἄνθραξ (anthrax, “charcoal, carbuncle”) in Greek. It occurs in Libya where the Troglodytes live.

4. St. Hildegard of Bingen — Physica, XIV. Carbuncle

Open the cited text

Carbuncle (carbunculus) develops during an eclipse of the moon. Then the moon is weary and wishes to fade, as sometimes it shows itself failing when, by divine order, it shows that there will be famine, plague, or changes of kingdoms. At that time, the sun sinks all its powers into the firmament. It places its tongue in the other’s mouth, so that it may resuscitate that which had died. It warms the moon with its heat, arousing and sustaining it with its fire, making the moon shine again. Carbuncle is born at this time. It has splendor from the sun’s heat, while it enlarges the moon, so it shines more at night than during the day. It grows until the sun’s heat casts it out. Since an eclipse of the moon is rare, this stone is rare. Its strength is unusual, and it should be feared and used with much reverence and concern.

If any illness has invaded a person, changing his humors, place a carbuncle on the sick person’s umbilicus at midnight. Its strength then is particularly vigorous. As soon as the person either feels himself warmed by the stone or feels the slightest motion in his body, remove the carbuncle from him. If you permit it to lie on his umbilicus any longer, its power will penetrate his whole body, drying it up. More than is possible for any unguent, its heat will have penetrated the person and all his internal organs. And so, this stone checks and dispels from humans any disease.

If someone’s head is ailing, place a carbuncle on the top of his head for a short time. When the flesh heats up, immediately take the stone away. The strength of the stone will penetrate his head better and more quickly than the most precious ointment or balsam. And so, his head will be better.

If you place this stone in clothing or any other thing, it will last longer and decay with difficulty. And, wherever there is a carbuncle, the airy spirits are unable to bring their phantasms to completion. They flee this stone, and turn away from it.

5. Marbodus of Rennes — De lapidibus, XXIII. Carbuncle

Open the cited text

The Carbuncle eclipses by its blaze All shining gems, and casts its fiery rays Like to the burning coal; whence comes its name, Among the Greeks as Anthrax known to fame. Not e'en by darkness quenched its vigour tires; Still at the gazer's eye it darts its fires; A numerous race, within the Lybian ground Twelve kinds by mining Troglodytes are found.

6. Camillo Leonardi — Speculum Lapidum

Open the Carbuncle passage · Open the Rubinus passage

Carbunculus [Carbuncle], which some call anthrax, shines forth fiery violet rays from every side and, because of it, shines like a fiery coal in the dark. Among the fiery gems, the carbuncle is the first for color, beauty, and price. There are twelve species. The most valued are found in Libya among the Troglodytes. Fire does not hurt it, nor does it absorb the color [light] of any other gem close to it, but other gems will absorb its color [light]. It is of male and female [gender]. Stars appear to be burning within, however the female stones emanate more light. Some say that the Indian ones are more valuable than all others. And though we have said that there are twelve types of this fiery stone, we will only mention the five we know. In the first place we should list the carbuncle, followed by the ruby and the balas, reputed to be of that species too. Rubith, similar to the spinel, is in fourth place, and in last place is the granate. The virtue of the carbuncle is of that of driving away pestilential and poisonous air, to quieten lust and induce and preserve good physical health. It drives away evil thoughts, reconciles fighting friends, and increases all prosperity.

Rubinus is a species of carbuncle, as already stated. Nor is it wholly different from it except for its size, and it has a similar virtue. There are two species: one we already mentioned; the other darker and of almost no value.

7. Bartholomaeus Anglicanus — De proprietatibus rerum, Book XVI, Chapter 26: Carbunculo

Open cited source

Carbunculus is a precious stone, and shines as Fire, whose shining is not overcome by night. It shines in dark places, and it seems as it were a flame. And the kinds thereof be twelve, and the worthiest be those that shine and send out beams, as it were fire, as Isidore says, libro. 16. cap. 3. There it is said that Carbunculus is called Antrax in Greek, and is gendered in Libya among the Troglodites. Among these twelve manner kinds of Carbuncles, those Antraciles be the best that have the colour of fire, and be compassed in a white vein, which have this property: If it be thrown in fire, it is quenched as it were among dead coals, and burns if water be thrown thereon. Another kind of a Carbuncle is called Scandasirus: and has that name of a place of India in the which it is found. In this manner of kind as it were within bright fires, be seen as it were certain drops of gold. And this precious stone is of great price without comparison in respect of other. Another manner of kind there is that is called Signs, & has that name of bright burning of lamps. And this precious stone is called Remissus Carbunculus. And there is two manner of kinds: That one is with beams, as purple, & that other is of the colour of red silk. And if this be heated in the Sun with rubbing of fingers, it draws to it self straw and leaves of books. It is said, that it with stands engraving. And if it be sometimes graued and printed with waxe, it takes with him a part of the waxe, as it were with biting of a beast, as Isidore says there. Among manner of kinds of Carbuncles, Balagius is counted, that is red and bright, as Dioscorides says: and also it is said, that this manner Carbuncle is found in the vein of the Sapphire: & therefore through neerenesse of the Sapphire, Balagius has a manner mist, as it were sparkling about the fire, that compasseth him without. And this is openly seen if men take heed thereto.

8. Thomas Cantimpre — Liber de natura rerum, Book XIV: “On Precious Stones”, Carbunculus — Carbuncle, Ruby, and Balas

Open cited source

The carbuncle is the most precious of all stones and one of the twelve said to have been placed on Aaron's vestment. Its colour is fiery. It shines more at night than by day, since its brilliance is obscured in daylight, but it dispels the darkness of night. In Greek it is called anthrax, and it is found in Libya, the land of the cave-dwellers.

There are three varieties: the first, carbuncle; the second, ruby; and the third, balas, which is of lesser worth. Ruby is fiery in colour, but incomparably less so than carbuncle; consequently, it does not dispel the darkness of night at all. It is also unequal to carbuncle in its powers. Nevertheless, among other stones it is more precious and more elegant, and has a star-like colour. Balas, which is the third variety, is much inferior in colour, power, and price, yet it is considered superior to sapphire and jasper.
