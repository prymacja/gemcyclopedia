Beryl

Beryl

The image shows Pale green beryl crystal (source).

NamesBeryl; ¹ ³ Beryllus; Berillus; ⁶ Crisopilon; Crisoberillus (a species name in the beryl entry); Berillo ⁷
AppearancePale, clear, and transparent or pale green; ¹ ³ ⁶ kinds also appear opaque, sea-colored, olive, dark, or blue-green. ² ⁶ Some contain a moving, starry or water-like light. ⁵ ⁷ ⁸
Found inIndia; ¹ ³ Mount Tauros and lands beyond the Euphrates; ² Babylon. ⁶ ⁷ ⁸

Beryl, also called Beryllus or Berillus, has many kinds, from pale and transparent to opaque, sea-green, olive, or dark. ¹ ⁶ Some contain a moving, water-like light and shine when cut with six faces. ⁵ Beryl is found in India, Tauros, beyond the Euphrates, and Babylon. ² ³ ⁶ Its virtues include victory, concord, healing, and the expulsion of poison; ⁴ its use in sunlight and its many named varieties are set out below. ¹

Generic

Description

Beryl has many kinds, and different accounts describe its color and clarity differently. Some are pale, clear, and transparent, with drops of water seeming to move within; ¹ others are opaque, sea-colored, pale green, olive, dark, or blue-green. ⁶ India produces rare white kinds with the color of seawater in sunlight, while other kinds are found at Tauros beyond the Euphrates or in Babylon. ² Some accounts count nine varieties. ³

The stone is commonly cut with six faces or into a hexagon, which enhances its gleam. ³ ⁵ When turned, water seems to move within it. ¹ ² Some kinds show luminous points like glass millet in sunlight; ² a ball-shaped beryl placed in direct sun kindles fire, while another account says a ball reflects the rays like a concave mirror. ¹ ⁶ Beryl is said to arise from water foam inflamed by the sun between the third hour and midday; ⁴ its nature is chiefly of air and water, with a little fire.

Subtype I: Pale Transparent Beryl

This pale, clear, transparent beryl appears to hold drops of moving water within it. ¹ ⁶ The paler specimens, especially those in which the drops are more visible, are considered better. It is produced chiefly in India. ⁶

Subtype II: Opaque Tauros Beryl

This kind is opaque, sea-colored, and airy, resembling Amethyst, Paiderot, and water-colored Hyacinth. ² When turned toward the sun, glass-like points shine within it. It is found on Mount Tauros beyond the Euphrates. ²

Subtype III: Dragon's-Pupil Beryl

One form resembles a dragon’s pupil. ² No color, locality, or separate virtue is specified for it.

Subtype IV: White-Wax Beryl

One beryl kind resembles white wax. ² The account gives no further distinction in color, place, or virtue.

Subtype V: Ice-Like Beryl

One form resembles something frozen. ² No further appearance or locality is given.

Subtype VI: Indian Green Beryl

The Indian kind is pale green like Emerald. ⁶ Its white kinds are like seawater seen in sunlight, and the Indian forms are especially transparent. ⁶ A hexagonal cut increases their gleam. ³ ⁵

Subtype VII: Catel

Catel is dusky and Crystal-colored, resembling Diamond. ⁶ It belongs among the beryl kinds favored by later collectors.

Subtype VIII: False Beryl

False beryl is paler and shines either gold-colored or blue-green; it is distinguished from the true beryl kinds and associated with Babylon. ⁶

Subtype IX: Crisopilon

Crisopilon is a paler beryl species, closely related to false beryl. ⁶ The account gives no separate locality or virtue.

Subtype X: Crisopassus

Crisopassus is named as a beryl species of golden color mixed with purple. ⁶

Subtype XI: Ysoberillus

Ysoberillus is named among the beryl species, but no particular appearance, locality, or separate virtue is given. ⁶

Subtype XII: Other Named Kinds

Other named forms include Hyacynthizonthes, heroides, and cervine kinds, as well as dark olive-colored and crystalline kinds. ⁶ The crystalline kinds resemble Crystal; no separate descriptions are given for the first three names. ⁶

Properties

Enemies, Disputes, and Conduct

Beryl protects against peril from enemies, overcomes disputes, and grants victory. It brings mildness of manner and cleverness. ¹ Carried, held, and looked upon frequently, it keeps its bearer tranquil and less given to quarrels. ⁴

Marriage, Friendship, Wealth, and Honors

Beryl makes husband and wife agree and strengthens their conjugal love. ¹ It brings wealth, honors, and victory over rivals. ⁵ Its wearer becomes solicitous and is freed from idle dreams when the stone is hung about the neck. ⁶

Poison and Bodily Ailments

When a person has eaten or drunk poison, grate a moderate amount of beryl into spring water or other water. ⁴ Drink it immediately and continue once daily on an empty stomach for five days; ⁴ the poison will pass through nausea or the bowels. ⁴ Beryl also counters sloth, pain in the liver and side, shortness of breath, belching, and watery eyes. ¹ ⁵

Water in which beryl has rested for a time is good for weak sight; ⁵ when drunk, it dispels sighs and heals liver disorders. ⁵ ⁶ Beryl heals and prevents illnesses of the throat, jaw, and mouth attributed to humidity of the head. Mixed with an equal quantity of silver, it heals leprosy. ⁶ It also aids pregnant women, preventing miscarriage and birth pains. ⁶

Sources

1. St. Albertus Magnus — The Book of Minerals, Tractate II

Open cited source

Beryllus (beryl) is a stone of a pale, clear, transparent colour; and therefore we have said above that, when it is turned about, water can be seen moving inside it. Mostly it is produced in India, as many other gems are. There are many kinds and varieties of this stone; but the better kind is said to be paler and to have more drops of water that can be seen moving inside it. It is said to be effective against peril from enemies and against disputes, and to give victory. It is also said to cause mildness of manner and to confer cleverness. Some medical men also say that it is good against sloth, and pains of the liver, and against shortness of breath and belching, and that it is good for watery eyes. For it is known by experience that when it is shaped into a ball and is placed in direct sunlight, it burns, and kindles fire. The goldsmiths also say that it makes husband and wife agree in marriage.

2. St. Epiphanius — On the Twelve Gemstones

Open cited source

The stone Beryl is opaque, sea-colored, air-like to amethyst and has the appearance like Paiderot and water-colored hyacinth. This is from the volcanic eruptions of the mountain that is called Tauros. If someone wants to turn it back and forth under the sun, luminous, glassy points like millet appear in its middle. And another Beryl is similar to the pupil of a dragon. And yet another Beryl is similar to white wax; and yet another Beryl to something frozen. But this Beryl is found beyond the river Euphrates, at Tauros.

3. St. Isidore of Seville — Etymologies XVI.vii.5

Open cited source

  1. Beryl (beryllus) originates in India, deriving its name from the language of the people of India. It is similar to a smaragdus in its greenness, but with a pale color. The Indians cut it into hexagonal shapes, so that its dull color is enhanced by the reflections of the faces; if cut in another way, it has no gleam. There are nine varieties.

4. St. Hildegard of Bingen — Physica, Stones

Open cited source

Beryl (beryllus) is hot and develops every day, between the third hour and midday, from foam of water, which the sun inflames mightily. Its power is more from air and water than from fire, although it is a little bit fiery.

If a person eats or drinks poison, grate a moderate amount of beryl into spring water, or any other kind of water. He should drink this immediately, and do the same thing for five days, drinking once a day on an empty stomach. The poison will either foam out through nausea or pass through his posterior.

One who always has beryl with him, who often holds it in his hand and looks at it frequently, is not easily at odds with other people, nor does he dispute, but rather he remains tranquil.

5. Marbodus of Rennes — De lapidibus

Open cited source

Cut with six facets shines the Beryl bright, Else a pale dulness clouds its native light; The most admired display a softened beam Like tranquil seas or olives' oily gleam. This potent gem, found in far India's mines. With mutual love the wedded couple binds; The wearer shall to wealth and honours rise And from all rivals bear the wished-for prize: Too tightly grasped, as if instinct with ire. It burns th' incautious hand with sudden fire. Lave this in water, it a wash supplies For feeble sight and stops convulsive sighs. Its species nine, for so the learned divide, Avail the liver and the tortured side.

6. Camillo Leonardi — Speculum Lapidum, Beryl and Engravings

Open Beryl passage

Berillus [Beryl] is an olive- or seawater-colored stone. They say there are nine types, all of which tend to a pale shade of green. It bears the name of the country, or the people, where it was first found, and it has a very beautiful hexagonal shape. India produces white beryls, the color of seawater struck by the rays of the sun; these are rarely found elsewhere. Curious collectors from antiquity valued most those similar to the seawater- and olive-colored varieties. The moderns, instead, value most those that have the color of a dusky crystal, since they are most similar to diamonds. Some call this type catel. There is another species, which is paler, and is called false beryl, which shines with a gold or blue-green color. These come from Babylon. Chrysopilon is close to it, but it is paler. These are followed by hyacynthizonthes, heroides, cervine, dark olive-colored ones, and crystalline ones, which are similar to crystal. But the Indian beryls are far more precious than all others since they are very transparent. And when these beryls are moved, it seems as if water inside them moves too. And this is the opinion of St. Albertus as well, though it differs from others. If these are shaped in the form of a ball and are exposed to the rays of the sun, they reflect these rays just like concave mirrors. This stone has a great virtue: it renders the wearer solicitous. It protects and increases conjugal love. Hung from the neck, it chases away idle dreams. It heals and prevents all illnesses of the throat, the jaw, and the mouth—that is, all those illnesses that originate from humidity of the head. If taken mixed with a similar quantity of silver, it heals leprosy. Water, in which it has been for a certain period, is good for the eyes, and if drunk it dispels sighs, and it heals disorders of the liver. It also aids pregnant women, preventing abortions and birth pains.

Open Frog and Hoopoe engravings

Frog; if this figure were sculpted on a beryl it has the virtue of reconciling enemies and bringing friendship among men if they were at odds with each other, when they are touched by it.

Hoopoe; if the figure of this bird, and the herb dragon arum [dragontea] in front of it, is found on a beryl, it will have the virtue of calling forth all water spirits and force them to speak; and to invoke one’s own dead to make them answer questions.

Open Dog engraving

Dog. The image of a leporini [sic] dog found engraved on beryl. Know that it is useful to gain very great honors, fame, and benevolence.

Open Crayfish engraving

Crayfish. This image engraved on beryl pacifies disputes and preserves love in a marriage.

7. Bartholomaeus Anglicanus — De proprietatibus rerum, Book XVI, Chapter 21: Berillo

Open cited source

Beryl is a stone of India, like in green colour to Smaragd: but it is with paleness, and is polished and shaped among the Indes in six corners shape: that dimness of colour may be excited by the rebounding of the corners. And otherwise shaped it has no bright seining. And of Beryl be ten manner kinds, as Isidore says, cap. de Viridibus Gemmis. Also there it is said, that Crisoberillus is a manner kind of Beryl. And the pale greennes thereof shines toward the colour of gold. And this stone comes to vs out of India. Dioscorides describes the virtues of Beryl and says, that among Beryl the stone that is most pals is best, the virtue thereof is good against peril of enemies, and against strift. And makes one so that he may not be overcome, and makes him mild, and gives good wit, and helps against diseases of the liver, and against fighthings and sobbings, and against bolkinges, and heals moist eyes, and burns his hand that bears it, if it be held before the Sun, & makes a man great of state, & joins wil love of matrimony.

8. Thomas Cantimpre — Liber de natura rerum, Book XIV: “On Precious Stones”, Berillus — Beryl

Open cited source

Beryl is one of the twelve stones. It has a pale colour like seawater. The best specimens, however, resemble clear oil, especially those that have no blemishes inside. There are very bright beryls which nevertheless have tiny, elongated markings within, like little hairs. If a beryl has a hexagonal form, it produces the colours of the rainbow when struck by a ray of sunlight. If it is round like an apple and moistened with water, in the heat of the sun it sets alight dead coals, blackened burnt linen, or dry growths on a tree.

Hence a certain versifier says:

The power that shapes me into fire has made me remarkable; I have received the sun's splendour, but not the fire's heat. Thus the eternal light protects from the heavenly height; It was incarnate without the mother being so defiled.

For it is said to cure the disease called quinsy, and by rubbing it on swellings of the glands arising from unhealthy humours it removes them while they are still increasing. It also reconciles the love of spouses and brings distinction to those who wear it. Washed in water, it benefits weak eyes. If that water is taken as a drink, it relieves belching, sighing, and pains of the liver. There are nine kinds of this stone, some of them as clear as crystal. India produces it.
