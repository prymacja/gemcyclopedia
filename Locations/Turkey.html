Jasper

Jasper, also called Jaspis or Iaspis, has many colors; ² ³ ⁴ ⁶ its most prized kind is translucent green with red veins and should be set in silver. ¹ ⁵ ⁶ It checks bleeding and fever, aids childbirth, and drives away harmful phantasms. Engraved figures carry further protections and powers.

Recorded localities: The Thermodon River (also called Thermodontos) and near Amatunt; ² Phrygia, Iberia, Hyrcania, Caspian lands, and Germany. It is also found in many places. ¹ ⁶ ⁷ ⁸

Turquoise

Turquoise, also called Turchois, Turchion, or Turchesia, is milk-veined: one account gives it a bright blue color, another a yellow-white hue. ¹ ² It is said to preserve sight and protect the bearer; ¹ ² horsemen carry it to keep their horses from tiring or suffering riding accidents.

Recorded localities: One account says the name comes from its country of origin but does not specify the country. ² ³

Phengites

Phengites is a hard, white, translucent stone associated with Cappadocia and the power to admit light through closed buildings. ¹ A source tradition identifies Specularis, also called Mirrorstone, with Phengites or Fongites; ² their other accounts describe glass-like sheets, three colored forms, and a red kind that eases bodily aches and anger when carried in the left hand. ³

Recorded localities: Cappadocia; ³ around Segovia or Segorbe in Spain; ¹ Teutonia; ² France; ² and Persia. ³

Corycus

Hephaestite

Hephaestite, also called Hephaestitis, is a ruddy, mirror-like stone that reflects images in red. ¹ Cast into boiling water, it stills the bubbling and cools the water; ² set in sunlight, it emits fiery rays and kindles dry matter. It protects crops from storms and blight, and guards its wearer from danger when worn over the heart.

Recorded localities: The Isthmus of Corinth; ² Corycus. ¹

Mount Taurus

Beryl

Beryl, also called Beryllus or Berillus, has many kinds, from pale and transparent to opaque, sea-green, olive, or dark. ¹ ⁶ Some contain a moving, water-like light and shine when cut with six faces. ⁵ Beryl is found in India, Tauros, beyond the Euphrates, and Babylon. ² ³ ⁶ Its virtues include victory, concord, healing, and the expulsion of poison; ⁴ its use in sunlight and its many named varieties are set out below. ¹

Phrygia

Haematite

Haematite, also called Hematite, Ematites, Emathitis, Emathites, Sanguineus Lapis, or Sedeneg, is a blood-veined, iron-colored stone that turns blood-red when ground. ³ ⁴ It preserves rather than corrodes the body and stops bleeding; ¹ its many remedies treat the eyes, wounds, discharges, snakebite, and bladder stones. ²

Recorded localities: Ethiopia, Arabia, Afriia, Egypt, Babylonia, Spain, Libya, Phrygia, and Germany. ¹ ² ³ ⁴ ⁵ ⁶

Sardis

Sard

Sard is a red, sometimes translucent stone associated with Sardis. ¹ ² ³ Its kinds differ in transparency and brightness; it is said to gladden the spirit, sharpen the mind, restrain bleeding, and counter the harmful influence of Onyx. The name is disputed: One account calls carnelian “sardius,” while one account explicitly distinguishes sardius from carnelian; see Carnelian. ⁴ ⁵

Recorded localities: Named for Sardis. ¹ ³ One account lists five regional kinds: Babylonian, Indian, Arabian, Egyptian, and Cyprian; ⁴ it may occur embedded in other mined stones like a heart. ⁶ ⁷

Troy

Sarcophagus

Sarcophagus is a body-eating stone from which some ancient coffins were made. ¹ ² ³ It consumes a corpse within thirty or forty days; one account says that only the teeth remain. ³ The stone is easily split along a line of cleavage and is found near Troy. ² In the East, a kindred stone can eat the flesh of a living person when bound to him. ²

Recorded localities: The region of Troy; ² similar flesh-eating rocks are found in the East.
